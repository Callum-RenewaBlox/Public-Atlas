Three words that make the rest easy

Each one appears on your bill.

kWkilowatt

How hard the building is drawing power at a moment, like the speedometer in a car.

A kettle draws about 3 kW while it boils.

kWhkilowatt-hour

How much electricity was used over time, like miles travelled. Most of the bill is charged per kWh.

One kW for one hour is one kWh.

kVAconnection size

How much power the grid connection is reserved to carry, like the seats booked in a hall. You pay for it every day.

Measured a little differently from kW: for this building, 1 kW needs roughly 1 to 1.1 kVA.

03
Where the electricity goes

Almost half of the electricity goes on things that run around the clock

We split the last 12 months into the always-on load — the level the building runs at overnight, which carries on through every hour of the day, busy or empty — and the extra electricity used on top of it at different times of the week.

!

Why the always-on load matters

About 70,000 kWh a year — roughly £15,000 at about 22p a kWh — goes on things that run around the clock: fridges, IT, water heaters and heating on standby. Every 2 kW found and switched off for good saves about 17,500 kWh a year.
i

Why weekdays matter

Weekday working hours, 8am to 5pm, use about 45% of all the electricity, a third of it on top of the always-on load. That is also when the café and room hire run, so it is the part to measure most carefully for VAT purposes.
04
Through the year

A winter day uses nearly two thirds more electricity than a summer day

A typical winter day uses about 580 kWh and a summer day about 354 kWh. Heating and lighting in the colder months add roughly 40,000 kWh a year, about a quarter of the total.

Use has dropped this year

August and September 2026 used 25% less than the same weeks of 2025, and April to August 2026 were the quietest months on record. The drop came from daytime use: nights are unchanged, so the always-on load is still there to find. If the drop lasts, it should shape the next contract.
i

The church year shows up too

Easter Sunday used 587 kWh, the busiest day of April, and the carol service on 14 December reached 66 kW. Boxing Day used 650 kWh, more than a normal weekday, holding about 30 kW from 7am to 11pm: worth checking the heating knows about bank holidays.
05
The size of the connection

The building pays to reserve about three times the power it ever uses

The grid connection is reserved at 250 kVA, and Citygate pays for that every day whether it is used or not. In 14 months the busiest half-hour reached 80 kW, which in connection terms is roughly 80 to 90 kVA. It is like booking a 250-seat hall every day when the most people who ever came was under 90.

What we recommend

Reduce the reserved capacity to 110 kVA. That is still a quarter to a third more than the busiest half-hour on record, and it lowers the daily capacity charge on every bill from then on. Confirm the busiest kVA figure on the invoices first.
i

Why it matters later too

Network charges are banded by connection size. At 250 kVA the church sits in the most expensive band for its type of connection. At 110 kVA it would qualify for a lower band when the network operator next reassesses.

What this could be worth

Four ways the data points to lower bills

Charity rate of VAT and no Climate Change Levy

Electricity used for the church’s charitable activities qualifies for 5% VAT (0% until 31 March 2027) and no Climate Change Levy. One declaration to the supplier switches both on. The exact figure depends on the charitable share your VAT adviser confirms.

£3,300–£6,500a yearReady now

Right-size the connection to 110 kVA

A smaller reserved capacity means a smaller capacity charge on every bill. The saving depends on the supplier’s rate per kVA.

Once the capacity is reduced, the site could move from the top band to a lower one when the network operator next reassesses bands. Timing and rules need confirming.
